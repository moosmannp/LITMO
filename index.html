Use case and data MOMo step 1

What the ontology is for, loosely described, and the data it must represent. Responsible: the whole team.

How this step works

Describe the problem deliberately loosely – a paragraph is enough, and it need not be crisp. It may cover a set of related use cases rather than one, and future extensions currently out of scope. Writing it this way keeps the goal a modular, extensible, reusable ontology suitable for adjacent purposes, not a one-off. Also name the data sources the ontology must represent or align against.

Literature optional

Papers, existing ontologies and concept tables become one concept inventory that the later steps build on. Skip this step if you have no such sources.

How this step works

Each paper is read on its own and condensed into a paper card: the concepts it defines (with definition, page and a verbatim quote), the relations and taxonomies it proposes, candidate competency questions and the vocabularies it uses. The cards are then merged into a concept inventory: which concepts several papers share, where their definitions disagree, and which themes could become modules. Existing ontologies and concept tables join the same inventory, so you see which concepts they already cover. Start with a pilot of 8–10 papers and check their cards before extracting the rest.

Competency questions MOMo step 2

Questions in natural language the finished knowledge graph should answer. After generation, each becomes a SPARQL test. Aim for about ten that probe different aspects. Responsible: the whole team.

How this step works

Competency questions refine the use case and check whether the data sources are adequate. Collect them from potential future users too, not only from the modelling team. Suggestions are limited to 10 sufficiently different questions; variations of one question add little.

Key notions and modules MOMo step 3

The few central concepts – typically 3 to 6 – that each become a module; the module's other classes and properties are modelled around them. Responsible: the whole team.

How this step works

A key notion is the origin point of a whole part of the ontology. Not every recurring noun is one: attributes, values, parts and participants belong inside a notion's module. A good test: would the team model it as its own module, with its own schema diagram? Watch for hidden concepts: "enslavement" is a momentary event, but being enslaved is a state with a duration – likely the real key notion. A forgotten notion can be added later.

Optional: suggestions for missing notions

The tool reads your use case and competency questions and proposes only notions that are missing from your list. Nothing is added until you accept it.

Design decisions MOMo step 4

What the generation builds on: a design pattern per module, a small set of general relations, and the vocabularies to reuse. Responsible: ontology engineers.

For each key notion, an existing ontology design pattern close or adaptable enough to serve as a template for its module. A notion may have several reasonable choices — a Recipe could be a Document, a Plan or a Process; the use case and questions decide. Without a fitting pattern the module is built from scratch.

Only selected patterns are passed to the generator; deselected notions are modeled from scratch.

A small vocabulary of general relations that every module uses, instead of each module inventing one narrow property per pair of classes. It is proposed from your competency questions, the key notions and the relations the literature states; the generation uses it once you approve it. Optional — small ontologies may do without.

Generate MOMo steps 5–7

The intensive, synchronous heart of the process. Key notions are modeled one at a time as modules, each with a schema diagram and documentation pairing the diagram with its formal axioms (MOMo 5–6); the module diagrams are then composed into a single ontology-wide diagram whose overriding priority is understandability (MOMo 7). Evaluation, refinement and the OWL file follow in step 9.
